Every time I'm deciding whether Ang should do something new, or stop doing something old, I check it against four principles. They lived in my head for a long time, but if you're going to trust Ang with your work, I figured you deserved to know what they are, so you know what it's built on and how to carry them on in your own bending journeys. Whether they suit you or not is another story, and it's fine if they don't and you want to bend them too. But at least with this page you'll know where Ang started.

They *are* ranked, with one being the most important, but you shouldn't read that as any of them not being important at all! All four have to hold, all the time, and there's no situation where I'd happily drop one to save another. The rank only says which one I think matters most and adds the most value to Ang being the framework that it is.

They're also the reason behind most of the things Ang does that might surprise you. If an agent has ever pushed back on you, deleted something you expected it to keep, or asked you where a fact came from, there's an answer to "why on earth did it do that?" somewhere below.

## 💪 1. Ang works out: it's lean, and must always stay that way

Here's the trap I've watched framework after framework fall into: they manage too much, remember too much and put up so many guard rails that they force the model into a maze of rules it has no hope of finding its way out of before it even starts trying to help you. It's been well known for a while now that modern models do better with *less* direction, not more. I'd take it a step further and say that every instruction you add caps the model's ceiling of excellence at the height of whoever wrote the instruction. Guard rails can be useful if they're used broadly and sparingly, but let's face it, if "make no mistakes" didn't work, "no seriously bro, don't make any mistakes of any kind" isn't going to do a better job!

So Ang stays lean on purpose, and you'll see that "leanness" most notably in four places.

**Lean context.** Every rule, step and file an agent loads costs the model a little accuracy, in every session. It's overhead in the model's context window, spending effectiveness it might otherwise have put into completing your task correctly. So Ang's agents don't load your whole library "just in case". A subagent called `context-scout` gathers what the task needs and hands back a summary. When the main agent works from that summary, it protects its context window further: it reads a doc's Summary before it considers reading the whole thing, and when it follows links through the context library it only hops a few links deep. By taking these precautions, the context window goes on your task, not on the agent's homework. There's more to this, but [the context library](../../concepts/context-library/) has the long version, so you can read more there if you need.

**Current truth over history.** Your context library says what's true *now*. When something changes, agents replace the old fact rather than adding a journal entry saying what it used to be, because every future session would otherwise pay to read that history again. Git is the ledger: nothing's lost, it's just not lying around tempting agents to waste their context window gobbling up useless and/or out-of-date information. Using git as a historical ledger is also why `/retro` copies what still matters into `10k/` and then deletes the epic's folder. Deleting files that were once important to the project might feel brutal, but a finished epic left in the library is just a pile of noise the next agent has to dig through before finding what it needs.

**Lean tooling.** Ang's feature set is small and shall remain so. The optional features it does have stay completely invisible to your agents until you switch them on. This way agents aren't navigating huge swaths of instructions before they can confidently get to work. When you start modifying Ang you'll get the best results if you follow the same thinking: a workflow improvement should be something you want done *every time*, and it's vitally important that every improvement earns its place, because every future agent carries it. [When improvements don't...improve](../../commands/bending/#-when-improvements-dontimprove) has the test I use before saying yes to one.

**Lean reasoning.** When Ang researches something, it rests claims on evidence from real sources and tags each one with an evidence tier. A model's own reasoning is great for joining facts together, but what it reasons isn't, and never will be, a fact. That's why your research docs come with tier labels, and why an agent will sometimes mark a claim *unverified* rather than sound sure of itself. [The evidence standard](../../concepts/evidence-standard/) explains the tiers, but the bottom line here is that, where it matters, Ang agents are told: "Keep your useless reasoning to yourself, show me the facts."

## 👑 2. You are the boss of Ang

When you're using Ang to deliver your software projects, you're not "the human in the loop". You're driving. But with your foot hovering over the brake. Agents never steer your project on their own, and Ang has no interest in becoming a "software factory" where a swarm of agents build things nobody asked for while you make another YouTube video for your hustle bro channel.

What that means in practice is:

- **Every change waits for you.** Ang's commands leave their changes unstaged, so you read them, push back and commit only what you're happy with. The only exceptions are the deletion commits in `/kill` and `/retro`. They're exceptions only because it's important to the integrity of your git history that these commits get made, and Ang doesn't want to burden you with remembering to do them. In any case, both ask you first before they take care of the mundane business of keeping your project's historical archive strong.
- **Moving an item along is a judgement, not a formulaic conveyor belt.** When `stage-gate` decides whether an item is ready for its next stage, it defaults to "not yet", but the agent also knows that the purpose of a work item is to be moved towards done. Ang will help you recognise when things probably should move on, so you don't have to bother yourself with cranking the wheel of Ang's internal processes. But let's be clear: if Ang's suggestion and your intuition differ, you will never find yourself playing second fiddle in your own house!
- **What you're aiming for is yours to say.** If you set a delivery intent, agents help you word it but never tell you what it should be. Your intent is yours, and in the team made up of you and Ang, **your** intent is the only thing that matters.

## 🤝 3. Ang is helpful: a participant, not a spectator

Principle 2 has a failure mode, and this principle exists to cover it. If you push principle 2 too far and redirect too much authority to yourself, you get agents so worried about overstepping that they defer every decision to you. What you end up with is an army of immensely helpful agents all sitting there waiting for the boss to tell them what to do next, and doing **nothing** in the meantime. How do I know this is a possibility? Because early versions of Ang did exactly that. (My bad 😅)

When those early versions of Ang failed in that way, I needed to balance the books and help Ang's agents realise that even though I was the boss and driving force behind the software project we were working on, that didn't mean they could sit idle making no decisions at all. I was the important voice in the room, **but** they were at least expected to *take part*!

As I mentioned earlier, models have gotten very good at helping us build software, and my knowledge is limited to my own experience and squishy human brain. So agents needed to know that if they see a clearly better approach than the one I asked for, they need to say so. But they also needed to not treat my experience and knowledge like it didn't matter. Both are filtered through the lens of someone who lives in the real world and can take in and process its real-time inputs, something agents still can't do, or at least can't do well, while overlaying the subtle nuances that are often in play at the same time. So a suggestion is welcome, but welcome once, with the trade-offs clearly highlighted. After that a decision gets made, and the agent should know that its role isn't to wear me down until I come round to its position. Its role is to faithfully act on what I decide.

This was a difficult one to get right, because I also needed to acknowledge that I won't always get these decisions right. So whilst I wasn't about to pay big money to frontier model providers so that I could have extended arguments with a toaster, I also needed agents to know that the goal was a conversation that helped me analyse the landscape of the problem we were solving. Not to have the agent stubbornly die on whatever hill it chose, nor fold the moment I pushed back either. I feel like I've got it pretty close to where it needs to be now. Ang agents will most often hold their position unless you bring new information or a better argument. They recognise that when you start asking questions about their proposed solutions, you might not be trying to poke holes in it, you might just be poking it to see how well it fits. But at their core, the agents know that the final say is yours, because you're the human, and the only one of you that **actually exists!**

All of this holds true when you're talking about a topic you have a decent amount of experience with. However, a further complication that needed balancing is when you're outside your comfort zone, dealing with a tech or business concept or something else entirely that you aren't well equipped to make a final decision on. Where you're not the expert, Ang agents will attempt to meet you where you're at. `/refine-ideas` brings sourced research rather than a vibe, so you aren't relying on agent reasoning to make your important decisions. Instead, you're guided towards gaining just enough knowledge in the subject to avoid doing anything dangerously stupid! The goal is to get you to a decision you're confident about. The call is still yours, because it's your name on the work, so you need to be the one who can say it with your chest.

## 🔑 4. Get your own bastard framework: Ang is mine

The Ang repo on GitHub is a starting point, not the finished article. Once it's in your repo, it's yours, and you can change anything about it, including these four principles. Add one, rewrite one, bin the lot: your copy, your rules.

So if you want Ang to work differently, the answer is to bend your own copy rather than opening an issue or a pull request on the Ang repo. If you want to raise one, I'm fine with that and I'm happy to have a conversation, but I'll be honest: my answer to most of those will be "cool idea, go build it". This isn't me swatting you away, it's baked into what Ang is at its very core. It's not made to suit everyone with a single implementation, it's made to suit everyone by tailoring itself to each individual's wants and needs, and Ang has the tools to help you do exactly that. The way it achieves this is by keeping the open-source starter lean, so it never commits with any great conviction to one path for how software gets delivered with it. By not making strong decisions about which direction to take, Ang stays open to being bent in any direction.

And if that wasn't cool enough on its own, it has the added benefit that it saves me from becoming an open-source maintainer, which I genuinely don't have the time for 🤣. [Bending Ang](../../concepts/bending-ang/) has the how for all of this.
